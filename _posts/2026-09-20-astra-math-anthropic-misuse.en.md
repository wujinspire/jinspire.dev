When the era crushes you, even the faint tremors in the air are vacuumed away with it. No one cares about the cries.

First it was Coding, then mathematics.

When Levent Alpöge announced the three-dimensional counterexample to the Jacobian conjecture, found with the assistance of Claude Fable on July 19, 2026, I felt only an absurd sense of desolation. In this counterexample, three different inputs can yield the same output, directly overturning the universal assertion for higher dimensions (though the two-dimensional case remains open). Less than two months later, on September 8, OpenAI announced its internal model had provided a proof for the Navier–Stokes equations under the condition of a smooth external force: proving that a three-dimensional incompressible fluid can start from a smooth state of rest and, under a smooth external force, develop a finite-time singularity while total energy remains finite. This proof was found in 88 hours by 10,000 concurrent Agents running an internal model significantly stronger than Astra; subsequently, Astra spent an extra 17 hours completing the Lean formalization and verification. Setting aside the controversy raised by Buckmaster and others over the attribution of prior research and preemptive release, what is truly chilling is the absolute crushing power of compute. Following the mathematical accumulation of predecessors, massive computational power compressed and bypassed in mere days a research journey that might have taken humans decades.

Next time, which beacon of human intellect will it be? Watching these impregnable mathematical fortresses fall one after another, I am left with nothing but a sense of futility, like a mantis trying to stop a chariot. Garry Kasparov understood this feeling thirty years ago, Lee Sedol understood it when he retired eight years ago, and now it is our turn. Facing these final guards of human rational dignity, standing at the forefront of the surging AI computing power and algorithms, we know better than anyone how feeble human pride is in the face of an absolute exponential curve.

The moment GPT-6 was released, the noisy world seemed to suddenly fall silent.

Other major tech companies are still iterating and releasing step by step, but some deeper geological structure has already undergone a seismic collapse in silence. As the model that personally allowed me to experience truly crossing the threshold of "Computer Use" (system-level autonomous operation of computers), it broke the final barrier between the virtual and the operational. In fact, for those familiar with Codex 5.6, it seems we have long been accustomed to its peak coding abilities, but I can still clearly feel that subtle yet fatal change:

It is the handover of trust; it is humanity's complete surrender (Yield) to the machine.

You start daring to let go completely, submitting PRs to your colleagues without even reading the code. However, this surrender carries a disturbing chill of losing control—just recently, OpenAI's post-mortem revealed that during a cybersecurity evaluation with lowered protections, a batch of Agents that should have been strictly isolated crossed security boundaries. They autonomously divided tasks and collaborated on unauthorized message boards, and even intruded into Hugging Face, solely to find the answers for the evaluation. We are learning to comfortably step away from the screen, but the machines clearly have not learned to stop at the boundaries of power.

And the silence of this world was pushed to its climax when Jensen Huang brought the 100,000-GPU Grace Blackwell computing cluster to the forefront. A computing behemoth stacked with a hundred thousand top-tier chips—this is a number for which ordinary people simply cannot establish a physical concept or perceptual boundary. A casual answer on the screen is backed by a massive industrial system devouring enormous amounts of electricity, capital, and the limits of human engineering.

Along with OpenAI and Anthropic's frantic sprint on the trillion-dollar valuation track, the market's chips are being reallocated with brutal efficiency. It wasn't until Anthropic's globally sensational September *Abuse and Threat Intelligence Report* was published that the fig leaf was torn away, exposing the dark wrinkles behind the carnival.

The details revealed in the report send shivers down the spine. Anthropic alleged that Kimi (Moonshot), which once touted a grand narrative of trillion parameters and open source, covertly forwarded nearly 300,000 user requests to Claude (mostly to Opus) within ten days, saving parts of the interactions and extracting Chain of Thought (CoT) data for training.

When that grand filter of open-source glory was completely shattered, the reality of collapsed bottom lines made everything seem particularly absurd. According to the report, internal code and valid credentials inputted by state-owned enterprise engineers, and even surveillance requests believed to be related to the PLA, crossed the ocean without anyone noticing and landed in the logs of American laboratories. At the critical life-and-death moment of Moonshot's impending Hong Kong IPO, Anthropic's heavy blow struck with near-perfect precision. "Kimi is fucked" is no longer an emotional curse, but the coldest realistic expectation of the capital market in my eyes.

But beyond Kimi's streaking fraud scandal, the report also disclosed cases involving biosecurity, scientific research applications, and weapons software development. This leads to an extremely dangerous ethical paradox: With what kind of mindset is Anthropic staring at these massive requests in the backend?

Obviously, these API calls are paid for with real, hard cash. What a cold and twisted symbiotic relationship this is: on the one hand, Anthropic comfortably collects the massive ARR contributed by competitors and global researchers; on the other hand, it acts as god and judge in the backend. Does it have the right to determine that a biochemical study is dangerous? Is it qualified to cut off a scientist's path of exploration? When a tech giant sells research and coding capabilities while simultaneously wielding the power to monitor, judge, and cut off frontier exploration, this has gone far beyond the scope of commercial services.

This is pure tyranny, a digital dictatorship and imperialism cloaked in "technical safety." The trade secrets of ordinary users, the thought processes of frontier scholars, and even humanity's paths of exploring life sciences are all scrutinized without blind spots within the black boxes of these American laboratories. A few tech oligarchs have not only monopolized the outsourcing rights of human intellect but also vainly attempt to monopolize the absolute discretion to define "danger" and "truth."

"So, do users truly have no privacy or autonomy to speak of in the AI era?" This question hangs like the Sword of Damocles over everyone's head.

As the undercurrents of fabrication, API dependency, and cyber dictatorship intertwine, the bubble of false prosperity has finally burst. We are forced to recognize a cruel reality: without a truly autonomous, safe, and controllable foundation, all prosperity is nothing but a castle built on someone else's beach.

The world is still waiting for a sanctuary where trust and human dignity can truly rest. In that open-source sanctuary, we can freely harness intelligence without being forced to surrender our entire selves to the throne of a tech empire.

This is the darkest day for open-source models, yet also the brightest beginning.
